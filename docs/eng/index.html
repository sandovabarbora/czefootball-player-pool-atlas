Atlas of the player pool

English players in Europe's 9 top-ranked leagues per million inhabitants, 2025/26

Research · Player pool atlas, English edition · England and 7 peers, 2025/26

3.60* players on 2025/26 rosters of Europe's 9 top-ranked leagues per million inhabitants (administrative count, any minutes)

Per head, England ranks 6th of 8 countries (England and 7 peers) for players in Europe’s top-ranked leagues. Beside that count sit 0.5 regular under-21 starters per club at home against 1.3 in France; and a median age of 22 at the first season with real playing time in a foreign league (n = 49).

The findings, the five pathway measures and the one-page brief summarise the atlas; each question page holds the evidence, and the methodology page the data, limitations and references.

In context

How one country’s professional pool compares with its peers, where its players move abroad and which leagues the national-team squad plays in, computed from public data on every run. The largest cohort gap is in midfielders aged 30+: 9 English players in the top-9 leagues against a peer median of 23. A recent English export (first top-9 season in the last two seasons) first reached a top-9 roster at a median age of 20 (n = 29); one from Spain at 21.5 (n = 98). Built from FBref, Wikipedia and Wikidata. England is the worked example; the pipeline takes a nationality code and a peer set.

* 208 ENG players who appeared (any minutes) for a club in the UEFA top-9 leagues in 2025/26, per 57.7 M inhabitants (Eurostat, 1 January 2024); peers counted the same way. With a floor of 450 minutes the count is 140 (2.43 per million, rank 7 of 8); with 900 minutes it is 114 (1.98, rank 6). Methodology.

The question and what this atlas adds

How many of a country’s players reach Europe’s top-ranked leagues, and how that number relates to the size of the country and to the path its players take, is usually studied in pieces. Research on player development looks inside clubs and national systems: at the environment of a Danish club with a record of producing senior players (Larsen et al., 2013), at how selection and de-selection shape who progresses in German talent promotion (Güllich, 2014), at the working relationship between youth and professional departments at elite European clubs (Relvas et al., 2010), and at selection biases such as the relative age effect in European youth football (Helsen, Van Winckel and Williams, 2005). Counts of players abroad are published by the CIES Football Observatory (Poli, Ravenel and Besson, 2024), without a per-head comparison of one nation against a fixed peer set. This atlas takes England as a worked example and puts, on one set of definitions and from public data only, the per-head count of players in the 9 top-ranked leagues beside measures of the path to them: youth minutes at home, the age and route of the first move abroad, playing time after it and the national-team squad. It is exploratory and descriptive and estimates no causal effect.

Findings

  1. 1

    English players with 450+ Big-5 minutes: 226 at the 1995/96 peak, 128 in 2025/26; most of them play in their own league.

    The change-point model’s most probable season for the fall is 2007/08 (posterior 11 %), a level change of ×0.95 (90 % HDI 0.77–1.17); that interval includes ×1.00, so the fall is not distinguishable from no change at the 90 % level. A break model dates a change in level; it identifies no cause.

    More on this

    The median age of the nation’s Big-5 players was 26 at the 1995/96 peak and 26 in 2007/08, when 17 players made a first Big-5 season of 450+ minutes.

  2. 2

    The per-head gap to France and Spain, split over three measured channels (descriptive).

    In a ridge fit over 8 countries, against France (2.19 per million) youth minutes go with −3.40 (90 % bootstrap interval −10.65 to +4.24), league strength with +9.71 (+1.94 to +13.65) and export age with −4.26, residual +0.14; against Spain (5.94 per million) youth minutes go with −1.06 (90 % bootstrap interval −3.31 to +1.35), league strength with +4.72 (+1.04 to +6.68) and export age with −6.39, residual +8.67.

    More on this

    The league-strength coefficient in this fit is negative (−51.1 per unit of the league multiplier): a weaker home league goes with more players in the top-ranked leagues per million, so a peer whose league is weaker than England’s (m_L 1.000) gets a positive league-strength contribution. The split describes an association across few, correlated national measures; it is not a causal accounting.

    Read the evidence →

  3. 3

    England’s own under-21 nationals’ share of home-league minutes over 6 seasons: a trend of −0.29 pp a season (90 % CI −0.78 to +0.21).

    The share by season, 2020/21 to 2025/26: 4.6, 5.2, 2.5, 4.3, 3.9, 3.0 %. An OLS line through the 6 seasons falls by 0.29 percentage points a season (90 % CI −0.78 to +0.21 pp), an interval that includes zero. The last step, 2024/25 to 2025/26, for the comparison countries and for other leagues whose share also fell by 2 points or more: France 8.7 → 8.8 %; Spain 7.8 → 4.8 %; Czechia 11.1 → 6.4 %; Poland 12.5 → 9.3 %.

    Read the evidence →

  4. 4

    Where England ranks among 8 countries on the pathway measures, 2025/26 (1 = highest share, youngest age, most moves).

    Under-21 share of home-league minutes 3.0 %, 7 of 8 (highest: Netherlands 13.6 %); regular under-21 starters per club 0.50, 7 of 8 (highest: Netherlands 1.89); median age at the first season with real playing time abroad 22 (n = 49), 1–3 of 8 from youngest (youngest: Belgium 22); first moves abroad in the covered seasons 49, 7 of 8 (most: Germany 316); home-league UEFA-coefficient multiplier ×1.00, 1 of 8; median share of club minutes for exports 33 %, 7 of 8. These are descriptive ranks on medians and counts without intervals, so neighbouring ranks can swap with a few players; none of them is shown to cause the per-head count.

    Read the evidence →

  5. 5

    Youth minutes in Germany and England after two academy reforms (descriptive).

    Germany after its DFB/DFL academy licensing (2001/02; Honigstein, 2015): its own under-21 nationals played 5 % of Bundesliga minutes the season before, 11 % at the highest point within twelve seasons (2009/10) and 6 % in 2025/26. England after its Elite Player Performance Plan (2012/13; Premier League, 2011): its own under-21 nationals played 3 % of Premier League minutes the season before, 6 % at the highest point within twelve seasons (2019/20) and 3 % in 2025/26. Each is one sequence in one country without a comparison group, so it describes timing and does not estimate an effect of either reform.
