The question and what this atlas adds

How many of a country’s players reach Europe’s top-ranked leagues, and how that number relates to the size of the country and to the path its players take, is usually studied in pieces. Research on player development looks inside clubs and national systems: at the environment of a Danish club with a record of producing senior players (Larsen et al., 2013), at how selection and de-selection shape who progresses in German talent promotion (Güllich, 2014), at the working relationship between youth and professional departments at elite European clubs (Relvas et al., 2010), and at selection biases such as the relative age effect in European youth football (Helsen, Van Winckel and Williams, 2005). Counts of players abroad are published by the CIES Football Observatory (Poli, Ravenel and Besson, 2024), without a per-head comparison of one nation against a fixed peer set. This atlas takes Denmark as a worked example and puts, on one set of definitions and from public data only, the per-head count of players in the 9 top-ranked leagues beside measures of the path to them: youth minutes at home, the age and route of the first move abroad, playing time after it and the national-team squad. It is exploratory and descriptive and estimates no causal effect.

  2. 2

    The per-head gap to Norway and Czechia, split over three measured channels (descriptive).

    In a ridge fit over 8 countries, against Norway (a gap of −3.21 per million) youth minutes go with −3.15 (90 % bootstrap interval −4.49 to −1.15), league strength with +3.49 (+1.27 to +4.91) and export age with 0.00, residual −3.55; against Czechia (a gap of −10.19 per million) youth minutes go with −7.41 (90 % bootstrap interval −10.31 to −2.34), league strength with −1.63 (−2.21 to −0.64) and export age with 0.00, residual −1.16.

    More on this

    The league-strength coefficient in this fit is negative (−56.8 per unit of the league multiplier): a weaker home league goes with more players in the top-ranked leagues per million, so a peer whose league is weaker than Denmark’s (m_L 0.636) gets a positive league-strength contribution. The export-age channel is zero by construction: Denmark, Norway and Czechia share the same median age at the first top-9 season among recent entrants (22). The split describes an association across few, correlated national measures; it is not a causal accounting.

  3. 3

    Denmark’s own under-21 nationals’ share of home-league minutes over 6 seasons: a trend of −0.32 pp a season (90 % CI −0.83 to +0.20).

    The share by season, 2020/21 to 2025/26: 16.0, 15.2, 16.0, 14.3, 13.2, 15.3 %. An OLS line through the 6 seasons falls by 0.32 percentage points a season (90 % CI −0.83 to +0.20 pp), an interval that includes zero. The last step, 2024/25 to 2025/26, for the comparison countries and for other leagues whose share fell by 2 points or more: Norway 10.5 → 11.5 %; Czechia 11.1 → 6.4 %; Poland 12.5 → 9.3 %; Spain 7.8 → 4.8 %.

  4. 4

    Where Denmark ranks among 9 countries on the pathway measures, 2025/26 (1 = highest share, youngest age, most moves).

    Under-21 share of home-league minutes 15.3 %, 1 of 8; regular under-21 starters per club 2.50, 1 of 8; median age at the first season with real playing time abroad 23 (n = 112), 2–4 of 9 from youngest (youngest: Norway 22.5); first moves abroad in the covered seasons 112, 1 of 9; home-league UEFA-coefficient multiplier ×0.37, 4 of 9; median share of club minutes for exports 45 % (90 % bootstrap interval 41–55 %, n = 75), 6 of 9 by the median; 31 % of the 150 players who went from the home league to a top-9 league since 2020/21 left from Nordsjælland or Midtjylland. These are descriptive ranks on medians and counts; apart from the minutes share they carry no interval, so neighbouring ranks can swap with a few players; none of them is shown to cause the per-head count.

  5. 5

    Youth minutes in Germany and England after two academy reforms (descriptive).

    Germany after its DFB/DFL academy licensing (2001/02; Honigstein, 2015): its own under-21 nationals played 5 % of Bundesliga minutes the season before, 11 % at the highest point within twelve seasons (2009/10) and 6 % in 2025/26. England after its Elite Player Performance Plan (2012/13; Premier League, 2011): its own under-21 nationals played 3 % of Premier League minutes the season before, 6 % at the highest point within twelve seasons (2019/20) and 3 % in 2025/26. Each is one sequence in one country without a comparison group, so it describes timing and does not estimate an effect of either reform.

Change log

  • 1 October 2026
    • The Showcase cards page no longer repeats the pool: the static cluster maps (the page's interactive atlas shows the same projections), the historical-analogs list (each card carries its analogs) and the player index (the pool list on the page and the Players page) were removed, about 7 000 words. Old links to them open the Players page.
    • Correction: each edition's Big-5 count applied the 450-minute floor to each club stint, not to a player's minutes summed across the five leagues as the method states. A player who moved between two Big-5 clubs during a season with fewer than 450 minutes at each was left out (England 2025/26: Ethan Nwaneri, 171 minutes at Arsenal and 324 at Marseille). The editions now count as the Nations page does and agree with it for every country and season: England 2025/26 is 128 (was 127), Spain's 1995/96 peak 324 (was 323), and Denmark, Croatia, Poland, Switzerland, France, Italy, Germany and others move by 1–3 players in some seasons (design/count-and-lono-protocol.md).
    • The series models were refitted on the corrected counts under the same convergence rule. Denmark's fit now fails it (one divergent transition after both sampler stages), so the Denmark edition and the Denmark rows of the Czech and Norwegian editions date no step; the pages say so. Every other dated season is unchanged: Czechia 2014/15 ×0.59 (90 % HDI 0.37–1.02), Germany 2000/01 ×0.75 (0.65–0.89). Backtest errors move by at most 0.35 players; the naive baseline's for Denmark is now 3.0 (was 2.7), Spain's 8.9 (was 8.6), Germany's 8.3 (was 8.6).
    • The leave-one-nation-out check of the export-age model now runs at the main fit's settings (four chains, 1000 draws) and meets the convergence rule in every edition (minimum bulk ESS was 177–324); the shift it reports moves by at most 0.0005. The page states its diagnostics.
    • The front page's header now carries the per-head figure and one sentence (the rank). The three other measures it listed are in the findings just below it; the counts at 450 and 900 minutes are on the Methodology page.
    • Each edition's own series model (the Big-5 page, its forecast and backtest, and the first finding on the Nations page) was refitted under a convergence rule written down on 1 October 2026, before the refit (design/edition-refit-protocol.md): four chains, 2000 tuning steps and 2000 draws, target acceptance 0.99 only where 0.95 failed; max R-hat at most 1.01, bulk and tail ESS at least 400, no divergent transitions. At the earlier settings (two chains, 500 draws) no edition's fit met it. Now every fit passes: the six home fits, the peers' fits, the forecast fits and every backtest origin.
    • What moved: England's fall, dated 2007/08 at ×0.95 (90 % HDI 0.77–1.17), is now ×0.96 (0.76–1.18), within 5 % of no change, so the England edition dates no fall (best candidate 2019/20, posterior 10 %). Germany's fall stays at 2000/01, ×0.75 (90 % HDI 0.64–0.90; was ×0.76, 0.65–0.98), posterior 75 % (was 69 %). Czechia's fall stays at 2014/15, ×0.59 (0.39–1.02; was ×0.60, 0.39–1.03), and its rise at 2000/01, ×1.67 (0.70–2.32; was ×1.65, 0.87–2.46). Every other dated season is unchanged. In Norway's backtest the model's mean error is now 3.4 players, the same as the naive baseline (was 3.2), so the model no longer beats it. The live forecasts move by at most two players; the registered forecasts are unchanged.
    • The Big-5 page of an edition that dates no step (England, Spain) no longer calls its best candidate season a rise or a fall in What this means.
    • The cross-edition change-point fits on the Nations page were refitted under a convergence rule written down before the refit (design/nations-refit-protocol.md): four chains, 2000 tuning steps and 2000 draws, target acceptance 0.95 and 0.99 only where needed; max R-hat at most 1.01, bulk and tail ESS at least 400, no divergent transitions. 15 of 17 fits pass and the page now lists every fit's diagnostics. Denmark and Switzerland fail (one divergent transition each) and are no longer dated.
    • Dated steps that moved in the refit: Poland's fall from 2014/15 to 2019/20 (×1.33, 90 % HDI 0.88–1.82) and its other step from 1998/99 to 2014/15; Italy's other step from 2010/11 to 2009/10. Every other country keeps its seasons with small changes in size. Czechia's fall stays at 2014/15, ×0.59, but its 90 % HDI widens from 0.38–0.97 to 0.37–1.02 and now includes no change; its rise stays at 2000/01, ×1.67 (0.74–2.40). The edition's own Big-5 fit, used in the findings, is unchanged.
