Atlas of the player pool

Danish players in Europe's 9 top-ranked leagues per million inhabitants, 2025/26

Research · Player pool atlas, Danish edition · Denmark and 8 peers, 2025/26

12.58* players on 2025/26 rosters of Europe's 9 top-ranked leagues per million inhabitants (administrative count, any minutes)

Per head, Denmark ranks 1st of 9 countries (Denmark and 8 peers) for players in Europe’s top-ranked leagues. Beside that count sit 2.5 regular under-21 starters per club at home against 1.6 in Norway; and a median age of 23 at the first season with real playing time in a foreign league (n = 112).

The findings, the five pathway measures and the one-page brief summarise the atlas; each question page holds the evidence, and the methodology page the data, limitations and references.

The question and what this atlas adds

How many of a country’s players reach Europe’s top-ranked leagues, and how that number relates to the size of the country and to the path its players take, is usually studied in pieces. Research on player development looks inside clubs and national systems: at the environment of a Danish club with a record of producing senior players (Larsen et al., 2013), at how selection and de-selection shape who progresses in German talent promotion (Güllich, 2014), at the working relationship between youth and professional departments at elite European clubs (Relvas et al., 2010), and at selection biases such as the relative age effect in European youth football (Helsen, Van Winckel and Williams, 2005). Counts of players abroad are published by the CIES Football Observatory (Poli, Ravenel and Besson, 2024), without a per-head comparison of one nation against a fixed peer set. This atlas takes Denmark as a worked example and puts, on one set of definitions and from public data only, the per-head count of players in the 9 top-ranked leagues beside measures of the path to them: youth minutes at home, the age and route of the first move abroad, playing time after it and the national-team squad. It is exploratory and descriptive and estimates no causal effect.

Findings

  1. 1

    Danish players with 450+ minutes in the Big-5 leagues: 38 at the 2025/26 peak, 38 in 2025/26.

    The change-point model’s most probable season for the rise is 2021/22 (posterior 23 %), a level change of ×1.41 (90 % HDI 0.88–1.97); that interval includes ×1.00, so the rise is not distinguishable from no change at the 90 % level. A break model dates a change in level; it identifies no cause.

  2. 2

    The per-head gap to Norway and Czechia, split over three measured channels (descriptive).

    In a ridge fit over 8 countries, against Norway (a gap of −3.21 per million) youth minutes go with −3.15 (90 % bootstrap interval −4.49 to −1.15), league strength with +3.49 (+1.27 to +4.91) and export age with 0.00, residual −3.55; against Czechia (a gap of −10.19 per million) youth minutes go with −7.41 (90 % bootstrap interval −10.31 to −2.34), league strength with −1.63 (−2.21 to −0.64) and export age with 0.00, residual −1.16.

    More on this

    The league-strength coefficient in this fit is negative (−56.8 per unit of the league multiplier): a weaker home league goes with more players in the top-ranked leagues per million, so a peer whose league is weaker than Denmark’s (m_L 0.636) gets a positive league-strength contribution. The export-age channel is zero by construction: Denmark, Norway and Czechia share the same median age at the first top-9 season among recent entrants (22). The split describes an association across few, correlated national measures; it is not a causal accounting.

  3. 3

    Denmark’s own under-21 nationals’ share of home-league minutes over 6 seasons: a trend of −0.32 pp a season (90 % CI −0.83 to +0.20).

    The share by season, 2020/21 to 2025/26: 16.0, 15.2, 16.0, 14.3, 13.2, 15.3 %. An OLS line through the 6 seasons falls by 0.32 percentage points a season (90 % CI −0.83 to +0.20 pp), an interval that includes zero. The last step, 2024/25 to 2025/26, for the comparison countries and for other leagues whose share fell by 2 points or more: Norway 10.5 → 11.5 %; Czechia 11.1 → 6.4 %; Poland 12.5 → 9.3 %; Spain 7.8 → 4.8 %.

  4. 4

    Where Denmark ranks among 9 countries on the pathway measures, 2025/26 (1 = highest share, youngest age, most moves).

    Under-21 share of home-league minutes 15.3 %, 1 of 8; regular under-21 starters per club 2.50, 1 of 8; median age at the first season with real playing time abroad 23 (n = 112), 2–4 of 9 from youngest (youngest: Norway 22.5); first moves abroad in the covered seasons 112, 1 of 9; home-league UEFA-coefficient multiplier ×0.37, 4 of 9; median share of club minutes for exports 45 % (90 % bootstrap interval 41–55 %, n = 75), 6 of 9 by the median; 31 % of the 150 players who went from the home league to a top-9 league since 2020/21 left from Nordsjælland or Midtjylland. These are descriptive ranks on medians and counts; apart from the minutes share they carry no interval, so neighbouring ranks can swap with a few players; none of them is shown to cause the per-head count.

  5. 5

    Youth minutes in Germany and England after two academy reforms (descriptive).

    Germany after its DFB/DFL academy licensing (2001/02; Honigstein, 2015): its own under-21 nationals played 5 % of Bundesliga minutes the season before, 11 % at the highest point within twelve seasons (2009/10) and 6 % in 2025/26. England after its Elite Player Performance Plan (2012/13; Premier League, 2011): its own under-21 nationals played 3 % of Premier League minutes the season before, 6 % at the highest point within twelve seasons (2019/20) and 3 % in 2025/26. Each is one sequence in one country without a comparison group, so it describes timing and does not estimate an effect of either reform.
